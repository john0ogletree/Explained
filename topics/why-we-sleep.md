# Why We Sleep

Sleep is the single most underrated thing you can do for your health. Not diet. Not exercise. Not supplements. Sleep. Every major system in your body — brain, immune, hormonal, cardiovascular — depends on it, and no amount of coffee or willpower can substitute for it.

And yet most people treat it as optional. Something to sacrifice when work gets busy. Something to "catch up on" over the weekend. Both of those instincts are wrong.

## What Sleep Actually Is

Sleep isn't one thing. It's a cycle of distinct stages, each doing different work.

- **N1** — the drift-off phase. Light, brief, easy to wake from.
- **N2** — the bulk of sleep. Memory consolidation, temperature drop, heart rate slowing.
- **N3** — deep sleep. Physical repair, immune function, growth hormone release.
- **REM** — dreaming. Emotional processing, creativity, pattern recognition.

A healthy adult cycles through these every ~90 minutes, 4–6 times a night. Cut the night short and you don't just lose "some sleep" — you lose the *later* cycles, which are disproportionately REM. That's why sleeping 5 hours isn't "almost 8." It's a different kind of sleep entirely.

> You can't make up lost sleep by sleeping in on Sunday. You can only avoid losing it in the first place.

## What Happens When You Don't Sleep

The effects are not subtle. After just one night of 4–5 hours:

- Reaction time drops measurably — comparable to being legally drunk.
- Emotional regulation collapses. Small things feel huge.
- Memory formation is impaired. What you learned that day doesn't stick.
- Appetite hormones shift. You feel hungrier, and you crave sugar.

After weeks of chronic short sleep:

- Immune function drops. You get sick more often.
- Blood sugar regulation worsens. Risk of type 2 diabetes rises.
- Cardiovascular strain increases. Blood pressure climbs.
- Cognitive performance declines across the board — attention, judgment, creativity.

After years:

- Higher risk of dementia, depression, obesity, heart disease.
- Shorter lifespan, consistently, across every study that's looked.

This isn't fringe. It's one of the most replicated findings in all of medicine.

## Why We Evolved to Sleep

Sleep is dangerous. An animal that's unconscious for eight hours is vulnerable to predators, can't find food, and can't reproduce. Evolution doesn't keep expensive traits unless they pay for themselves.

So why did it stick around? Because sleep is doing something the waking brain can't.

- **Memory consolidation.** The hippocampus replays the day's experiences during sleep, transferring what matters into long-term storage.
- **Metabolic cleanup.** The glymphatic system flushes waste from the brain — including the proteins implicated in Alzheimer's — mostly during deep sleep.
- **Emotional processing.** REM lets you re-experience difficult memories without the full stress response, which is how trauma gets integrated.
- **Immune reset.** Cytokines and T-cells are produced and organized during sleep. Skip it and you get sick.

Every one of those functions is essential. None of them can happen while you're awake.

## What Modern Life Does to Sleep

We've built a world that's hostile to sleep. Not on purpose, but effectively.

- **Artificial light** after sunset delays melatonin release.
- **Screens** keep the brain alert and suppress the wind-down signal.
- **Caffeine** has a half-life of 5–6 hours. A 3pm coffee is still active at 9pm.
- **Alcohol** knocks you out but fragments REM all night.
- **Irregular schedules** — shift work, late weekends, jet lag — confuse the circadian clock.
- **Anxiety** keeps the mind scanning for threats when it should be offline.
- **Temperature** — most bedrooms are too warm for optimal sleep.

None of these are moral failures. They're just the default environment. Changing them takes deliberate effort.

> The modern world didn't make sleep impossible. It made it inconvenient — and then told you that was fine.

## What Actually Helps

Most sleep advice is noise. These are the things that hold up.

- **Same wake time every day.** More important than bedtime. Anchors the circadian rhythm.
- **Morning light.** Ten minutes outside within an hour of waking. Sets the whole day's clock.
- **Cool, dark, quiet room.** Around 65°F, blackout curtains, no ambient noise.
- **No caffeine after noon.** Half-life math makes this non-negotiable for most people.
- **No alcohol within 3 hours of bed.** Even one drink fragments the second half of the night.
- **Wind-down routine.** Same sequence every night. Signals to the brain that sleep is coming.
- **Get out of bed if you can't sleep.** Lying there frustrated trains your brain to associate bed with anxiety.

Notice what's *not* on this list: apps, trackers, supplements, weighted blankets, "sleep hygiene" checklists longer than the above. Those can help at the margin. The fundamentals above do almost all the work.

## The Cultural Problem

We treat sleep as laziness. People brag about sleeping 5 hours like it's a virtue. Workplaces reward staying late and punish leaving on time. Students pull all-nighters before exams and wonder why they can't remember anything.

This is backwards. Sleep is when your brain and body do the work that makes the rest of the day productive. Cutting it doesn't make you more effective — it makes you slower, sicker, and less able to think.

The people who perform at the highest levels over decades — athletes, researchers, writers — almost universally treat sleep as a pillar, not a luxury. The ones who brag about not sleeping tend to burn out.

> "I'll sleep when I'm dead" is a plan to get there faster.

## A Simple Framework

If you take nothing else from this:

1. Pick a wake time. Keep it every day.
2. Get light in your eyes in the morning.
3. Cut caffeine early. Cut alcohol close to bed.
4. Keep the room cool and dark.
5. Treat the 30 minutes before bed as a wind-down, not a to-do list.

Do those five things for two weeks and you'll sleep better than 90% of people you know. Not because it's a hack — because it's the baseline your body was designed for, and almost nobody is running it.
