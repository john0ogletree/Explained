# How Credit Cards Work

A credit card is a short-term loan you can use anywhere. You swipe, tap, or type the number, the purchase goes through, and you promise to pay the bank back later. That's the basic idea.

What's actually happening underneath is stranger. Four parties are involved in every transaction, the money moves through a system built in the 1960s, and the "credit" isn't really credit — it's a revolving line that most people never fully pay off.

## The Four Parties

Every card transaction involves four parties. Understanding them explains almost everything about how cards work.

- **The cardholder** — you, the person making the purchase.
- **The merchant** — the business accepting the card.
- **The issuing bank** — the bank that gave you the card. Chase, Citi, Capital One, your credit union.
- **The acquiring bank** — the merchant's bank, which receives the funds on their behalf.
- **The card network** — Visa, Mastercard, Amex, Discover. They don't issue cards or hold money. They run the rails.

When you tap your card, the merchant sends a request through their acquirer, which routes it through the card network, which asks your issuing bank: "does this person have the money?" The issuing bank says yes or no. That's the authorization.

## Authorization vs. Settlement

The tap-and-go moment feels like the money has moved. It hasn't. That's just **authorization** — a hold, a promise, a check.

**Settlement** happens later, usually overnight. That's when the actual money moves from your issuing bank to the acquiring bank to the merchant's account. It's invisible to you, but it's where most of the complexity lives.

> When you see a "pending" charge on your statement, that's authorization. When it turns into a "posted" charge, that's settlement.

## Where the Money Actually Goes

For a $100 purchase, roughly this happens:

- The merchant receives about **$97–$98**.
- The **card network** takes a small cut — a few cents.
- The **issuing bank** takes the **interchange fee** — typically 1.5% to 3% of the transaction.
- The **acquiring bank** keeps a small margin.

That gap is called the **merchant discount rate**. It's why some small businesses offer cash discounts or charge a minimum for card use — they're paying for the privilege of accepting cards.

## Why Interchange Exists

The interchange fee is the single most misunderstood part of the system. It's not a tax. It's a **payment for risk and service** from the merchant's bank to the cardholder's bank.

The issuing bank:
- Takes on the risk that you won't pay
- Provides fraud protection to you
- Handles disputes and chargebacks
- Funds rewards programs (cash back, points, miles)
- Extends credit in the first place

That's why premium rewards cards charge merchants more. Someone has to pay for the airport lounge.

> Every "free" rewards program is funded by the merchant, and ultimately by the customer.

## Credit vs. Debit vs. Charge

These three get mixed up constantly.

| Type | Where the money comes from | When you pay |
|---|---|---|
| **Debit** | Your checking account | Immediately |
| **Credit** | A line of credit from the bank | Later, with interest |
| **Charge** | A line of credit, paid in full monthly | Every month |

Credit cards let you carry a balance. Charge cards (like the classic Amex) traditionally don't. Debit cards pull directly from your bank and offer weaker fraud protection in some cases.

## The Interest Trap

If you pay your full statement balance every month, you pay **zero interest**. The card is effectively free money for 25–55 days.

If you don't, the math turns against you fast. Typical APRs run 20–30%. Compounded monthly. On a $2,000 balance at 24%, that's roughly $40 a month just in interest — with nothing to show for it.

Minimum payments are designed to maximize that. Paying the minimum on a $2,000 balance at 24% APR takes **over a decade** to clear, and you'll pay thousands in interest along the way.

> A credit card is a 30-day interest-free loan if you use it well, and one of the most expensive loans in existence if you don't.

## Fraud Protection

Credit cards have strong legal protections in most countries. In the US, the **Fair Credit Billing Act** limits your liability to $50 for unauthorized charges — and in practice, most issuers cover it entirely.

This is the one genuinely good reason to use credit over debit for online purchases. If your debit card is compromised, the money leaves your account immediately and you have to fight to get it back. With credit, it's the bank's money that's missing, not yours.

## Rewards Programs

Cash back, points, miles — they're all marketing. Whether they're worth it depends on one thing: **do you pay your balance in full every month?**

If yes, rewards are free money. Take them.

If no, you're paying 24% interest to earn 2% back. That's a bad trade, and the rewards exist precisely because enough people make it.

## How to Use a Credit Card Well

- Pay the full statement balance every month
- Treat the credit limit as a ceiling, not a budget
- Keep utilization low — under 30% is a common rule of thumb
- Never use a credit card for money you don't already have
- Set up autopay for at least the minimum, to avoid late fees
- Check your statement monthly for fraud
- Don't chase rewards that require spending more than you planned

The card is a tool. Used well, it's convenience, fraud protection, and free rewards. Used badly, it's a debt trap with a nice design.

## The Honest Summary

Credit cards are:

- A short-term loan with strong consumer protections
- Funded by merchants, not by banks, through interchange
- Free if you pay in full, brutal if you don't
- A payment network designed decades ago, still largely unchanged
- Neither inherently good nor bad — it depends entirely on how you use them

> The system is built to profit from people who carry a balance. The trick is to be someone who doesn't.
