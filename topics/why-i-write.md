# Why I Write

I write because I forget things. Not big things — small things. The shape of an argument I had six months ago. The reason a project failed. The version of me that thought a particular idea was obvious and didn't need writing down. All of it drifts away unless I catch it somewhere.

Writing is how I catch it.

## The First Reason: Compression

Most of what I know is compressed into a feeling. I sense that something is true before I can say why. Writing is the process of unpacking that feeling into something another person could check.

If I can't explain it in writing, I don't understand it. That's not modesty — it's a test. A vague intuition survives in my head forever because nothing challenges it. Once it's on the page, it has to survive contact with logic, with counterexamples, with the reader who'll disagree.

Half the things I "know" turn out to be half-thoughts when I try to write them down. That's the value.

## The Second Reason: Memory

I have a terrible memory for my own past. Not events — conclusions. I forget what I believed, why I changed my mind, what I tried that didn't work.

Writing is a message to future me. When I open an old note and find an argument I no longer agree with, that's useful. It means I've moved. If I read something from two years ago and still nod along, that's useful too — it means the idea held up.

Either way, I only know because I wrote it down.

> The person I was last year is a stranger. Writing is how I keep in touch with him.

## The Third Reason: It's Slow

Writing is slower than thinking. That's the point.

Thinking races. It skips steps, makes leaps, and arrives at conclusions that feel earned but aren't. Writing forces every step to happen in order. You can't skip a premise. You can't hand-wave a transition. The sentence either works or it doesn't.

This slowness is uncomfortable. It exposes weak reasoning that thought was happy to hide. But it's also the only way I've found to actually think rather than feel like I'm thinking.

## The Fourth Reason: It's Public

I used to keep everything private. Notes, drafts, half-finished essays. All of it for an audience of one.

Then I started publishing. Not because I had something important to say, but because publishing changes the writing. Once someone else might read it, I have to be honest in a different way. I can't get away with the same shortcuts.

This isn't about validation. It's about accountability. A private note can be wrong without consequence. A public one can be wrong in a way that requires a correction, a revision, an admission. That pressure makes the writing better.

> Writing for yourself lets you lie. Writing for others forces you to notice when you do.

## The Fifth Reason: It's How I Learn

I've never understood a topic fully until I've written about it. Reading gives me familiarity. Writing gives me fluency. They're different states.

The moment I try to explain something to a reader, every gap in my understanding lights up. The things I thought I knew become questions. The connections I assumed became claims I have to justify. I end up learning the topic twice: once when I read, again when I write.

That's why this site exists. Every topic here is a topic I wanted to understand, and writing it was the last step.

## What Writing Isn't

It isn't productivity. Writing rarely feels productive. Most sessions end with fewer words than I started with.

It isn't therapy. It can be, occasionally, but that's a side effect, not the point.

It isn't mastery. I've written about things I later discovered I had wrong. The writing didn't prevent that — it just made the wrongness visible sooner.

It isn't for everyone. Some people think clearly without writing. I'm not one of them, and I stopped pretending otherwise.

## Why I Keep Doing It

The honest answer is: I don't know.

I don't write for money. I don't write for attention. I don't write because I think the world needs more essays. I write because not writing feels worse. Because the alternative is a head full of half-formed ideas that never get tested. Because at the end of a good session, something in me settles that nothing else settles.

Maybe that's the whole answer. Writing isn't something I do. It's something I am, on the days I'm paying attention.

> The reason to write isn't that you have something to say. It's that you don't know what you think until you've said it.
